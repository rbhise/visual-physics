Laws of Motion, lesson 3

Uniform and Non-uniform Motion

Watch a car on an empty highway, then one stuck in city traffic. One covers the same ground every second. The other never does.

Download slides (.pptx) 12 slides with teacher notes

Uniform motion

An object is in uniform motion when it covers equal distances in equal intervals of time, however small those intervals are. Its speed does not change. When this happens along a straight line, we call it uniform linear motion.

In the simulation, car A moves uniformly. The dots it leaves each second are evenly spaced.

Non-uniform motion

An object is in non-uniform motion when it covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time. Its speed keeps changing: a bus pulling away from a stop, a scooter braking for a signal, a car in heavy traffic.

Car B moves non-uniformly. The gaps between its dots grow, shrink or jump around.

How to spot it: look at the distance covered in each second. If every second gives the same distance, the motion is uniform. If not, it is non-uniform.

What the graph shows

The graph under the cars plots distance against time. Uniform motion gives a straight line, because the same distance is added every second. Non-uniform motion gives a curve or a line that bends. Lesson 5 looks at these graphs in detail.

Read the table

Two cars start together. Their distances from the start are recorded every second.

Time (s)012345
Car P (m)01530456075
Car Q (m)0515305075

Car P adds 15 m every second, so it moves uniformly. Car Q adds 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 m, so it moves non-uniformly. Both cover 75 m in 5 s, so they have the same average speed of 15 m/s. Only car P actually travels at that speed the whole time.

Try it

Car A always moves uniformly. Change how car B moves and compare the dots and the graph.

  • Speeding up. How do B's gaps change?
  • Slowing down. B starts fast and brakes to a stop.
  • Heavy traffic. B moves, stops, then moves again.

What you should see: car A's dots stay evenly spaced and its graph is a straight line. Speeding up, B's gaps grow; slowing down, they shrink; in traffic, some gaps are zero (B is standing still) and its graph has flat parts. With A at 10 m/s, both cars reach 100 m at 10 s in the first two runs, yet only A moved uniformly.

Worked examples

1. An express train

An express train covers 25 m in every second for 2 minutes. Is its motion uniform? What is its speed, and how far does it travel?

  1. Equal distances (25 m) in equal times (each second): the motion is uniform
  2. Speed \(= 25\ \text{m} \div 1\ \text{s} = \) 25 m/s
  3. Time \(= 2 \times 60 = 120\) s, so distance \(= 25 \times 120 = \) 3000 m (3 km)

2. Car Q from the table

Using the table above, find car Q's average speed over 5 s, and the distance it covered in the first and in the last second.

  1. Average speed \(= 75 \div 5 = \) 15 m/s
  2. First second: \(5 - 0 = \) 5 m
  3. Last second: \(75 - 50 = \) 25 m

Car Q's speed changed from about 5 m/s to about 25 m/s, so 15 m/s is only its average.

Common mistakes

  • Judging by the total only. Two cars can cover the same total distance in the same time. Check the distance in each equal interval.
  • Thinking “uniform” means “fast”. A tortoise walking at a steady 0.1 m/s is in uniform motion. Uniform means unchanging, not quick.
  • Ignoring stops. A stop is part of the motion. A car that stops at a signal is in non-uniform motion.

Quick check

1. A cyclist covers 4 m, 4 m, 4 m and 4 m in four seconds, one after another. Her motion is…

Equal distances in equal time intervals means uniform motion.

2. Which of these is most likely to be non-uniform motion?

In traffic, a car speeds up, slows down and stops, so it covers unequal distances in equal times.

3. On a distance–time graph, uniform motion appears as…

The same distance is added in every second, so the points lie on a straight line.

4. Two runners both finish 100 m in 20 s. Which statement is true?

Average speed is 100 ÷ 20 = 5 m/s for both. That says nothing about how their speed changed during the race.
