Distance–Time and Velocity–Time Graphs

A graph tells the whole story of a journey at a glance: when it was fast, when it slowed down, when it stopped. You just need to know how to read it.

Download slides (.pptx) 12 slides with teacher notes

Distance–time graphs

Time goes along the horizontal axis and distance up the vertical axis. Each point tells you how far the object has gone at that moment.

The slope (steepness) of a distance–time graph is the speed: the distance gained divided by the time taken. In the simulation, the dashed coral line touches the graph at the current moment and its slope is shown in the readout.

  • Straight sloping line: uniform motion. A steeper line means a higher speed.
  • Flat line: the distance is not changing, so the object is at rest.
  • Curve getting steeper: speeding up. Curve getting flatter: slowing down.

Velocity–time graphs

Now velocity goes up the vertical axis. The slope of a velocity–time graph is the acceleration, and the area under the graph is the distance travelled.

  • Flat line above zero: uniform velocity, zero acceleration.
  • Straight line sloping up: uniform positive acceleration.
  • Straight line sloping down: uniform negative acceleration (deceleration).
  • Flat line at zero: at rest.
Why the area is the distance: for uniform velocity the area is a rectangle, velocity × time, which is exactly the distance. In the simulation, the teal area always equals the distance in the readout.

Try it

Play a journey, or drag across the graphs to move through it. Compare the two graphs at the same moment.

  • Steady speed. What shape is each graph?
  • Speeding up. Which graph is curved, and which is straight?
  • Stop at a signal. Find the part where the car is at rest on both graphs.
  • A train between stations. Read the acceleration from the velocity graph.

What you should see: at steady speed the distance graph is a straight line and the velocity graph is flat. Speeding up, the distance graph curves upward while the velocity graph is a straight sloping line. At the signal, the distance graph goes flat and the velocity graph drops to zero for 2 s. For the train, the area under the velocity graph is 84 m, the same as the final distance.

Worked examples

1. Reading a distance–time graph

A cyclist's distance–time graph is a straight line from (0 s, 0 m) to (5 s, 30 m). Describe her motion and find her speed.

  1. A straight sloping line means uniform motion
  2. Speed = slope \(= \dfrac{30 - 0}{5 - 0} = \) 6 m/s

2. A train between two stations

A train starts from rest and reaches 12 m/s in 3 s. It runs at 12 m/s for 4 s, then slows uniformly to rest in 3 s. Find its acceleration in each part and the total distance.

  1. Speeding up: \(a = (12 - 0)/3 = \) 4 m/s²
  2. Steady part: \(a = \) 0
  3. Slowing down: \(a = (0 - 12)/3 = \) −4 m/s²
  4. Distance = area = \(\tfrac12(3)(12) + 4(12) + \tfrac12(3)(12) = 18 + 48 + 18 = \) 84 m

Common mistakes

  • Reading the graph as a map. A distance–time graph is not a picture of the road. A curve does not mean the road bends.
  • Mixing up the two flat lines. Flat on a distance–time graph means at rest. Flat on a velocity–time graph means steady speed.
  • Taking the slope of a v–t graph as velocity. The slope of a velocity–time graph is acceleration. The slope of a distance–time graph is speed.
  • Forgetting triangles. When working out area, split the shape into rectangles and triangles (½ × base × height).

Quick check

1. A distance–time graph is a flat horizontal line. The object is…

The distance does not change with time, so the object is not moving.

2. What does the slope of a velocity–time graph tell you?

Slope = change in velocity ÷ time taken, which is acceleration.

3. A velocity–time graph is a flat line at 5 m/s from 0 to 4 s. How far does the object travel?

Area = 5 × 4 = 20 m.

4. A distance–time graph curves and gets steeper and steeper. The object is…

A steeper slope means a higher speed, so the speed is increasing.
