Laws of Motion, lesson 8

Newton's Laws of Motion

Why does a bus passenger lurch forward when the driver brakes? Why does a gun kick back when it fires? Three laws, written by Isaac Newton in 1687, explain both.

Download slides (.pptx) 13 slides with teacher notes

Balanced and unbalanced forces

A force is a push or a pull. When several forces act on an object, what matters is their combined effect, the net force.

  • Balanced forces cancel out, so the net force is zero. In a tug of war where both teams pull equally hard, the rope does not move.
  • Unbalanced forces leave a net force. The object's motion changes: it starts, stops, speeds up, slows down or turns.

The crate in the simulation slides on smooth ice, so there is no friction. Only the two pushes act along the ice.

First law: inertia

An object continues to remain at rest or in a state of uniform motion along a straight line unless an external unbalanced force acts on it.

This tendency to keep doing what it is already doing is called inertia. The more mass an object has, the more inertia it has.

  • Inertia of rest: flick a card from under a coin on a glass, and the coin drops into the glass instead of moving with the card.
  • Inertia of motion: when a bus brakes suddenly, passengers lurch forward because their bodies keep moving.
  • Inertia of direction: when a car turns sharply, passengers feel pushed to the side because their bodies try to keep going straight.

Second law: force and momentum

The momentum of an object is its mass times its velocity, \(P = mv\), measured in kg·m/s. Newton's second law says that the rate of change of momentum is proportional to the applied force, and the change happens in the direction of the force. For a fixed mass this becomes:

$$F = ma$$

The unit of force is the newton (N): the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s². So \(1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^2\).

A cricketer catching a fast ball pulls his hands back. The ball's momentum still drops to zero, but over a longer time, so the force on his hands is smaller.

Third law: action and reaction

Every action force has an equal and opposite reaction force, and the two act at the same time.

The two forces act on different objects, so they never cancel each other out.

  • A balloon pushes air out backwards; the air pushes the balloon forwards.
  • A gun pushes the bullet forwards; the bullet pushes the gun back, so the gun recoils.
  • A rocket pushes hot gases down; the gases push the rocket up.
  • When you walk, your foot pushes the ground backwards; the ground pushes you forwards.

Try it

Each set-up uses a 20 kg crate. Look at the net force and what happens to the velocity.

  • Tug of war. 50 N each way, crate at rest.
  • Push from rest. 40 N to the right only.
  • Balanced while moving. 30 N each way, crate already sliding at 2 m/s.
  • Push against the motion. 20 N to the left on a crate sliding right at 3 m/s.

What you should see: in the tug of war nothing moves. Pushing from rest, the crate accelerates at 2 m/s² and reaches 20 m/s after 10 s. Balanced while moving, it keeps sliding at exactly 2 m/s, because no net force means no change in velocity. Pushing against the motion, it slows at 1 m/s², stops after 3 s, then speeds up to the left.

Worked examples

1. Pushing a crate

Two people push a 20 kg crate on smooth ice: one with 40 N to the right, the other with 10 N to the left. Find the acceleration.

  1. Net force \(= 40 - 10 = 30\) N to the right.
  2. \(a = F/m = 30/20 = \) 1.5 m/s² to the right

2. A car pulling away

What force is needed to give a 1200 kg car an acceleration of 1.5 m/s²?

  1. \(F = ma = 1200 \times 1.5 = \) 1800 N

3. Catching a cricket ball

A 0.15 kg ball arrives at 20 m/s. A fielder stops it in 0.1 s. Find the force on his hands. What if he pulls his hands back so that stopping takes 0.3 s?

  1. Change in momentum \(= 0.15 \times 20 - 0 = 3\) kg·m/s.
  2. Force = change in momentum ÷ time \(= 3 / 0.1 = \) 30 N
  3. With 0.3 s: \(3 / 0.3 = \) 10 N, a third of the force.

Common mistakes

  • “Action and reaction cancel.” They act on different objects. The gun is pushed back and the bullet forwards; neither force cancels the other.
  • “A moving object needs a force to keep moving.” With no friction it keeps moving by itself. Forces are needed to change motion, not to keep it going.
  • Ignoring direction. Forces in opposite directions subtract. Find the net force before using F = ma.
  • Mixing grams and kilograms. F = ma needs mass in kg. A 150 g ball is 0.15 kg.

Quick check

1. A book lies still on a table. The forces on it are…

Gravity pulls it down and the table pushes it up equally. The net force is zero, so it stays at rest.

2. When a bus starts suddenly, standing passengers fall backwards. This is due to…

Their bodies tend to stay at rest while the bus moves forward beneath them.

3. A net force of 12 N acts on a 4 kg trolley. Its acceleration is…

\(a = F/m = 12/4 = 3\) m/s².

4. A swimmer pushes water backwards with her hands. The reaction force…

The water pushes back on the swimmer with an equal and opposite force, at the same time.
