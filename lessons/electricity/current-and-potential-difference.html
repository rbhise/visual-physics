Current Electricity, lesson 1

Current and Potential Difference

Flick a switch and a bulb lights at once. Nothing races round the wire at high speed; instead, countless electrons start drifting together. What pushes them, and how do we measure their flow?

Download slides (.pptx) 12 slides with teacher notes

Electric charge

Electrons carry a tiny negative charge. Charge is measured in coulombs (C). The charge on one electron is \(1.6 \times 10^{-19}\) C, so one coulomb is the charge of about \(6.25 \times 10^{18}\) electrons.

In a metal wire, some electrons are only weakly held by their atoms. These free electrons can move through the wire.

Potential and potential difference

Water flows from a higher level to a lower level, and keeps flowing only while there is a difference in level. Electric charge behaves in a similar way: it flows between two points when there is a difference in their electric potential.

The potential difference between two points is the work done to carry a unit positive charge from one point to the other:

$$V = \frac{W}{Q}$$

Its unit is the volt (V): \(1\ \text{V} = 1\ \text{J/C}\). Smaller and larger units are the millivolt (1 mV = 10⁻³ V) and the kilovolt (1 kV = 10³ V). A cell keeps a potential difference between its terminals, just as a pump keeps one tank higher than the other. Switch to the Water analogy view to see this.

Electric current

Electric current is the charge passing through a conductor in unit time:

$$I = \frac{Q}{t}$$

Its unit is the ampere (A): 1 A = 1 C of charge passing each second. Smaller units: the milliampere (1 mA = 10⁻³ A) and the microampere (1 µA = 10⁻⁶ A). In the simulation, start the clock: the readout counts the charge passing and divides by the time.

Which way does current flow?

Electrons are negative, so they drift from the negative terminal of the cell, through the circuit, to the positive terminal. Before electrons were discovered, scientists had already agreed that current flows from positive to negative. We still use this conventional current, opposite to the electron flow.

In the simulation: teal dots are electrons (− to +). Coral arrows show conventional current (+ to −).

Circuit symbols

Circuit diagrams use standard symbols so that anyone can read them. An ammeter is always joined in series, so the current passes through it. A voltmeter is joined in parallel, across the two points whose potential difference it measures.

Try it

Start the clock in each case and compare the charge that passes.

  • One cell. 1.5 V across the bulb.
  • Four cells. 6 V across the same bulb.
  • Switch open. Four cells, but the circuit is broken.
  • Water with no pump. What happens to the flow?

What you should see: with one cell the current is 0.25 A, so 2.5 C passes in 10 s. With four cells the current is 1 A: four times the charge every second, and a brighter bulb. With the switch open, nothing flows however many cells you use. Without the pump, the water levels even out and the flow stops.

Worked examples

1. Charge from current

A current of 0.3 A flows through a torch bulb for 10 minutes. How much charge passes through it?

  1. \(t = 10 \times 60 = 600\) s
  2. \(Q = I \times t = 0.3 \times 600 = \) 180 C

2. Potential difference from work

12 J of work is done in moving 4 C of charge between two points. Find the potential difference between them.

  1. \(V = W / Q = 12 / 4 = \) 3 V

3. Counting electrons

How many electrons make up a charge of 1 C? How many pass each second when the current is 2 A?

  1. \(n = \dfrac{1}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = \) 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons
  2. 2 A means 2 C every second: \(2 \times 6.25 \times 10^{18} = \) 1.25 × 10¹⁹ electrons per second

Common mistakes

  • Minutes instead of seconds. I = Q/t needs t in seconds to give amperes.
  • Mixing up current and potential difference. Potential difference is the push (volts); current is the flow (amperes).
  • Electrons flow from + to −. Electrons drift from − to +. Conventional current is the one taken from + to −.
  • Ammeter across a component. An ammeter goes in series; a voltmeter goes in parallel.

Quick check

1. A charge of 60 C passes through a wire in 2 minutes. The current is…

\(I = 60 / 120 = 0.5\) A. Remember 2 minutes = 120 s.

2. 1 volt is the same as…

Potential difference is work per unit charge: 1 V = 1 J/C.

3. Conventional current flows…

Conventional current is taken from the positive terminal round to the negative terminal, opposite to the electrons.

4. How should a voltmeter be connected to measure the potential difference across a bulb?

A voltmeter is joined across (in parallel with) the component.
