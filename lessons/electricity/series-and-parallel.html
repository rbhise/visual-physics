Resistors in Series and Parallel

When one bulb in an old string of festival lights fails, the whole string goes dark. When one bulb fails at home, the rest stay on. The difference is how they are connected.

Resistors in series

Resistors are in series when they are joined end to end, so there is only one path for the current.

$$R_s = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \dots$$
  • The same current flows through every resistor.
  • The potential differences across the resistors add up to the total: \(V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3\).
  • The effective resistance is larger than any single resistor. Series connection is used to increase resistance.
  • If one resistor breaks, the circuit is broken and the current stops everywhere.

Resistors in parallel

Resistors are in parallel when they are joined between the same two points, so each has its own path.

$$\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3} + \dots$$
  • The same potential difference is across every resistor.
  • The current divides between the branches, and the branch currents add up to the total: \(I = I_1 + I_2 + I_3\).
  • The effective resistance is smaller than the smallest resistor. Parallel connection is used to reduce resistance.
  • If one resistor breaks, the others keep working.

Try it

Use the readouts to check the rules above.

  • Two in series. 4 Ω and 8 Ω across 12 V.
  • Two in parallel. 6 Ω and 3 Ω across 6 V.
  • Break one in series. Same series circuit, R₂ broken.
  • Break one in parallel. Three in parallel, R₂ broken.

What you should see: in series, Rs = 12 Ω and 1 A flows through both; the 12 V splits into 4 V and 8 V. In parallel, Rp = 2 Ω; the 6 Ω branch takes 1 A and the 3 Ω branch 2 A, 3 A in total. Breaking a series resistor stops all current. Breaking one parallel branch leaves the other two working, though the total current falls.

Worked examples

1. Two resistors in series

Resistors of 4 Ω and 8 Ω are joined in series to a 12 V battery. Find the effective resistance, the current and the potential difference across each.

  1. \(R_s = 4 + 8 = \) 12 Ω
  2. \(I = V / R_s = 12 / 12 = \) 1 A
  3. \(V_1 = 1 \times 4 = \) 4 V; \(V_2 = 1 \times 8 = \) 8 V (together 12 V)

2. Two resistors in parallel

Resistors of 6 Ω and 3 Ω are joined in parallel across a 6 V battery. Find the effective resistance and the current in each branch.

  1. \(\dfrac{1}{R_p} = \dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{3}{6}\), so \(R_p = \) 2 Ω
  2. \(I_1 = 6 / 6 = \) 1 A; \(I_2 = 6 / 3 = \) 2 A
  3. Total \(= 3\) A, and check: \(6 / 2 = 3\) A ✓

3. Three in parallel

Find the effective resistance of 10 Ω, 20 Ω and 20 Ω joined in parallel.

  1. \(\dfrac{1}{R_p} = \dfrac{1}{10} + \dfrac{1}{20} + \dfrac{1}{20} = 0.1 + 0.05 + 0.05 = 0.2\)
  2. \(R_p = 1 / 0.2 = \) 5 Ω, less than the smallest resistor (10 Ω)

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to flip at the end. In parallel you first find 1/Rp. Take the reciprocal to get Rp.
  • Adding parallel resistors. R₁ + R₂ is only for series. Parallel always gives a smaller value.
  • Same current in parallel. In parallel the voltage is shared equally, not the current.
  • Expecting voltages to add in parallel. In parallel each resistor has the full battery voltage.

Quick check

1. Resistors of 2 Ω, 3 Ω and 5 Ω are joined in series. The effective resistance is…

In series, add them: 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 Ω.

2. Two 10 Ω resistors are joined in parallel. The effective resistance is…

1/Rp = 1/10 + 1/10 = 1/5, so Rp = 5 Ω.

3. In a parallel circuit, what is the same for every resistor?

Each branch is joined between the same two points, so each has the same potential difference.

4. Why are household appliances connected in parallel?

Parallel connection gives every appliance the full supply voltage and its own independent path.
