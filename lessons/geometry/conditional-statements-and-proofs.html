Basic Concepts in Geometry, lesson 2

Conditional Statements and Proofs

Geometry is built from statements of the form “if this, then that”. Learn to split them into the given part and the part to be proved, to turn them round into a converse, and to prove them.

Conditional statements

Many properties can be written in the form “If …, then …”. Such a statement is called a conditional statement.

Antecedent and consequent. In a conditional statement, the part after “if” is the antecedent: it is the given part. The part after “then” is the consequent: it is the part to be proved.

“Every number divisible by 4 is divisible by 2” becomes:

If a number is divisible by 4, then it is divisible by 2.

Picture it with sets: the statement is true when everything that fits the antecedent also fits the consequent. In the simulation the violet set (divisible by 4) sits completely inside the teal set (divisible by 2).

The converse

Converse. When the antecedent and the consequent of a conditional statement are interchanged, the new statement is called the converse of the first statement.

The converse of the statement above is “If a number is divisible by 2, then it is divisible by 4.” This is false: 6 is divisible by 2 but not by 4. An example that shows a statement is false is called a counterexample. So the converse of a true statement is not always true. Sometimes it is: “If a triangle is equilateral, then its angles are all equal” and its converse are both true.

Proofs

A theorem is a statement that is proved by logical steps. We write what is given (the antecedent), what is to be proved (the consequent), and then the proof. Each step must follow from the given facts, from definitions, or from properties already accepted or proved.

  • Direct proof. Start from the given facts and move step by step to the conclusion. Example: when two lines intersect, the vertically opposite angles are equal.
  • Indirect proof. Suppose the conclusion is false. Show that this leads to a contradiction: something that cannot happen, or that goes against the given facts or an accepted property. Then the supposition is wrong, so the conclusion is true.

Examples, however many, cannot prove a general statement. But one counterexample is enough to show that a statement is false.

Worked examples

1. Write in if-then form

Write “A number ending in 0 is divisible by 5” as a conditional statement. Name the antecedent and the consequent, and write its converse.

  1. If a number ends in 0, then it is divisible by 5.
  2. Antecedent: a number ends in 0. Consequent: it is divisible by 5.
  3. Converse: If a number is divisible by 5, then it ends in 0. It is false: 15 is divisible by 5 but ends in 5.

2. Is the converse true?

“If two angles form a linear pair, then they are supplementary.” Write the converse and decide whether it is true.

  1. Interchange the parts: “If two angles are supplementary, then they form a linear pair.”
  2. Counterexample: angles of 130° and 50° drawn far apart. They add up to 180°, but they are not adjacent, so they are not a linear pair.
  3. The converse is false

3. Using a direct proof

Lines AB and CD intersect at O and \(\angle AOC = 55^\circ\). Find \(\angle BOC\), \(\angle BOD\) and \(\angle AOD\).

  1. \(\angle AOC\) and \(\angle BOC\) form a linear pair, so \(\angle BOC = 180^\circ - 55^\circ = \) 125°
  2. Vertically opposite angles are equal: \(\angle BOD = \angle AOC = \) 55°
  3. \(\angle AOD = \angle BOC = \) 125°

Common mistakes

  • “The converse must be true too.” Not always. Test the converse separately; one counterexample shows it is false.
  • Proving with examples. A few examples that work do not prove a general statement. You need a proof.
  • Swapping the parts by mistake. The antecedent is the part after “if”; the consequent is the part after “then”.
  • Supposing the wrong thing. In an indirect proof, keep the given facts and suppose that the conclusion is false.

Quick check

1. In “If a triangle is equilateral, then it is isosceles”, the antecedent is…

The antecedent is the given part, after “if”.

2. The converse of “If x = 4, then x² = 16” is…

Interchange the antecedent and the consequent. (This converse is false: x could be −4.)

3. “If two angles are right angles, then they are congruent.” Its converse is…

The converse is “If two angles are congruent, then they are right angles”. Two 40° angles are a counterexample.

4. An indirect proof begins by…

We suppose the conclusion is false and show that this leads to a contradiction.
