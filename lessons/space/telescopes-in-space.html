Observing Space: Telescopes, lesson 3

Telescopes in Space

Stars twinkle, which is pretty but a nuisance for astronomers. The twinkling, the clouds and the X-rays that never reach the ground are all down to one thing: the air above us.

Problems with the atmosphere

  • It absorbs radiation. Gamma rays, X-rays and most ultraviolet are absorbed high up (ultraviolet largely by the ozone layer); much infrared is absorbed lower down by water vapour. Even visible light is dimmed.
  • It moves. Layers of warm and cool air keep shifting and bend starlight this way and that. Stars twinkle and telescope images blur.
  • Weather and light. Clouds block the view, the bright daytime sky hides the stars, and city lights brighten the night sky.

Radio waves pass through the air, clouds and all, and can be observed by day, so radio telescopes like the GMRT work well on the ground.

Going higher

One answer is to build observatories on high, dry mountains, above much of the air and water vapour. India's observatory at Hanle in Ladakh is about 4500 m above sea level. The complete answer is to put the telescope above the atmosphere, in orbit around the Earth. There it can observe every wavelength, day and night, with no blurring.

Space telescopes are expensive and hard to repair, so ground telescopes are still used wherever the atmosphere allows: for visible light from mountain tops and for radio waves almost anywhere.

Try it

Choose a kind of radiation and a place for the telescope.

  • Visible at sea level. Watch the star image.
  • Visible from space. Compare the image.
  • X-rays from the ground. What arrives?
  • Infrared on a mountain. Does going higher help?

What you should see: At sea level the star image shimmers and blurs; from space it is a sharp, steady point. X-rays are stopped about 80 km up, so a ground telescope detects nothing. Much infrared is absorbed by water vapour low down, so a high, dry mountain does much better than sea level, though space is best.

Worked examples

1. Choosing a site

An astronomer wants to study X-rays from hot gas around a black hole. Where must her telescope be, and why?

  1. X-rays are absorbed high in the atmosphere, about 80 km up.
  2. The telescope must be in space, for example Chandra or Astrosat.

2. The GMRT's collecting area

Each GMRT dish is 45 m wide. Find the total collecting area of its 30 dishes.

  1. One dish: \(\pi r^2 = 3.14 \times 22.5^2 \approx 1590\) m²
  2. Thirty dishes: \(30 \times 1590 \approx\) 47 700 m², about seven football fields

3. Signals from Hubble

Hubble orbits about 550 km above the ground. How long do its radio signals take to reach a station directly below?

  1. Radio waves travel at \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s
  2. \(t = 550\,000 \div 3 \times 10^8 \approx\) 0.0018 s (under 2 milliseconds)

Common mistakes

  • “Space telescopes are closer to the stars.” 550 km is nothing compared with the distance to a star; the gain is getting above the air.
  • “Stars really flicker.” Twinkling is caused by moving air in our atmosphere.
  • “Radio telescopes must be in space.” Radio waves pass through the atmosphere, even clouds.
  • “Ground telescopes are useless now.” They are cheaper, bigger and easy to repair, so they are still widely used.

Quick check

1. Which of these is the main reason X-ray telescopes are put in space?

X-rays never reach the ground.

2. Why do stars twinkle?

Shifting air in our atmosphere bends starlight in changing directions.

3. Astrosat was launched by…

Astrosat is India's first dedicated space observatory, launched by ISRO in 2015.

4. Which kind of telescope works well on the ground, even through clouds?

Radio waves pass through the atmosphere and clouds.
