Observing Space: Telescopes, lesson 1

Light We Can and Cannot See

Stars and galaxies send us far more than the light our eyes can see. To study them, astronomers collect radiation of every wavelength, from gamma rays to radio waves.

Download slides (.pptx) 12 slides with teacher notes

Electromagnetic waves

Light is an electromagnetic wave. Unlike sound, it does not need a medium: it crosses the empty space between the stars and us. All electromagnetic waves travel through a vacuum at the same speed,

$$c = 3 \times 10^8 \ \text{m/s}$$

As with sound, speed, frequency and wavelength are linked:

$$c = \nu \lambda$$

So a shorter wavelength means a higher frequency.

The spectrum

Arranged by wavelength, electromagnetic waves form the electromagnetic spectrum. Our eyes detect only a narrow part of it, visible light, with wavelengths from about 400 nm (violet) to 800 nm (red). (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m.) The rest is invisible to us, but each kind can be detected with the right instrument.

Why use them all?

The same object can look completely different at different wavelengths. Dust clouds that hide newborn stars in visible light glow in infrared; the hottest gas shines in X-rays; cold hydrogen gas between the stars is seen only in radio. Observing at many wavelengths gives the whole picture.

Our atmosphere lets only visible light, radio waves and a little infrared reach the ground. Gamma rays, X-rays and most ultraviolet and infrared radiation are absorbed high up. That is good for life on Earth, but it means telescopes for those wavelengths must go above the atmosphere (lesson 3).

What you should see: Green light (500 nm) has ν = 6 × 10¹⁴ Hz and gets through the air. 1 nm X-rays have a frequency of 3 × 10¹⁷ Hz and are absorbed high up. A 3 m radio wave has ν = 10⁸ Hz (100 MHz) and reaches the ground easily. 100 µm infrared is absorbed by water vapour in the air.

Worked examples

1. Frequency of green light

Green light has a wavelength of 500 nm. Find its frequency.

  1. \(\lambda = 500 \times 10^{-9} = 5 \times 10^{-7}\) m
  2. \(\nu = c / \lambda = 3 \times 10^8 / 5 \times 10^{-7} = \) 6 × 10¹⁴ Hz

2. An FM radio station

A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz (1 MHz = 10⁶ Hz). What is the wavelength?

  1. \(\lambda = c / \nu = 3 \times 10^8 / 1 \times 10^8 = \) 3 m

3. How many times longer?

Compare a 1 m radio wave with a 1 nm X-ray.

  1. \(1 \text{ m} \div 10^{-9} \text{ m} = 10^9\)
  2. The radio wave is a billion times longer, yet both travel at \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s.

Common mistakes

  • “Radio waves are sound.” Radio waves are electromagnetic, like light. A radio set turns them into sound.
  • “X-rays travel faster.” All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum.
  • Mixing up the direction. Shorter wavelength means higher frequency.
  • “Space is dark except for visible stars.” Space is full of radiation our eyes cannot detect.

Quick check

1. The range of wavelengths of visible light is about…

Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to 800 nm (red).

2. Which of these travels fastest through empty space?

All electromagnetic waves travel at 3 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum.

3. A radio wave has a frequency of 3 × 10⁹ Hz. Its wavelength is…

λ = 3 × 10⁸ ÷ 3 × 10⁹ = 0.1 m.

4. Which radiation from space reaches the ground easily?

The atmosphere has 'windows' for visible light and radio waves.
