Observing Space: Telescopes, lesson 2

Optical and Radio Telescopes

In 1609 Galileo pointed a small telescope at the sky and saw mountains on the Moon and moons around Jupiter. Every telescope since has done the same two jobs: gather more light than the eye, and show finer detail.

Why a telescope?

A distant star or galaxy is so faint that the little light entering the pupil of your eye (about 7 mm wide at night) is not enough to see it. A telescope collects light over a much larger area and brings it to a focus. A wider telescope also shows finer detail. The width of its main lens or mirror is called its aperture, D.

Light gathered depends on area. Area goes as the square of the width, so a telescope 10 times wider than your pupil collects \(10^2 = 100\) times as much light. Doubling the aperture gathers four times the light.

Refracting telescopes

A refracting telescope uses lenses. A large convex lens at the front, the objective, refracts the parallel rays from a star to a focus. A small lens near the eye, the eyepiece, magnifies the image. Galileo's telescope was of this kind.

Big lenses cause problems: glass is heavy and a lens can only be held at its edge, so it sags; different colours focus at slightly different points, giving coloured fringes; and the glass must be perfect all the way through. The largest refractor ever built is only about 1 m wide.

Reflecting telescopes

A reflecting telescope uses a concave mirror instead of the objective lens. Mirrors can be supported from behind, need a perfect surface on one side only, and reflect all colours to the same focus. So every large modern telescope is a reflector.

  • Newtonian: designed by Isaac Newton. A concave mirror at the back reflects the light towards a focus; a small plane mirror at 45° turns it out through the side of the tube to the eyepiece.
  • Cassegrain: a small convex mirror reflects the light back down the tube through a hole in the centre of the concave mirror, to an eyepiece or camera at the back. The tube can be much shorter.

Radio telescopes

A radio telescope is a large dish shaped like a parabola, often made of metal mesh. Like a concave mirror, it reflects the radio waves falling on it to a receiver at its focus, and computers turn the signals into images. Radio waves are very long, so radio dishes must be huge. India's Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (GMRT) near Narayangaon, about 80 km from Pune, has 30 dishes, each 45 m wide, spread over 25 km and working together.

Try it

Choose each design and follow the rays.

  • Refracting. Where is the focus?
  • Newtonian. Which way does the light leave the tube?
  • Cassegrain. Where does the eyepiece go?
  • A radio dish. Where is the receiver?

What you should see: In the refractor the objective focuses the light near the back and the eyepiece sends a narrow parallel beam to the eye. The Newtonian sends the light out through the side. The Cassegrain sends it back through a hole in the main mirror to the eye at the back. The dish brings radio waves to the receiver at its focus.

Worked examples

1. A small telescope

A telescope has an aperture of 14 cm. How many times more light does it gather than your eye (pupil 7 mm)?

  1. Ratio of widths \(= 14 \div 0.7 = 20\)
  2. Light gathered \(\propto D^2\): \(20^2 = \) 400 times

2. Doubling the aperture

An observatory replaces a 50 cm mirror with a 100 cm one. How much more light does it collect?

  1. Width doubles, so area grows by \(2^2 = \) 4 times

3. The Hubble Space Telescope

Hubble's main mirror is 2.4 m wide. Roughly how many times more light does it gather than your eye?

  1. \(240 \div 0.7 \approx 343\)
  2. \(343^2 \approx\) 1.2 lakh (about 120 000) times

Common mistakes

  • “A telescope is just a magnifier.” Its first job is to collect light, so faint objects become visible.
  • “Twice as wide, twice the light.” Light gathered depends on area: twice as wide means four times the light.
  • Mixing up the two reflectors. Newtonian: plane mirror, eyepiece at the side. Cassegrain: convex mirror, eyepiece at the back.
  • “Radio telescopes listen to sounds.” They detect radio waves, which are electromagnetic, not sound.

Quick check

1. Which telescope uses a concave mirror and a plane mirror?

Newton's design turns the light out of the side with a flat mirror at 45°.

2. Why are all very large telescopes reflectors?

Mirrors avoid the weight and colour problems of big lenses.

3. A telescope's aperture is tripled. The light it gathers becomes…

Light gathered goes as D², and 3² = 9.

4. Where is the GMRT?

The Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope is near Narayangaon, about 80 km from Pune.
