Projectile Motion

Anything thrown, kicked or launched follows a curved path. That curve is two simple motions happening at the same time.

The big idea

Once a projectile leaves your hand, the only force on it (ignoring air resistance) is gravity, and gravity pulls straight down. Nothing pushes it sideways, so its horizontal velocity never changes. Its vertical velocity changes at a steady rate of \(g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\) downward.

Turn on Components in the simulation and launch. The teal arrow stays exactly the same length for the whole flight. The coral arrow shrinks to zero at the top, then grows pointing down.

Treat x and y as two separate problems. The only thing they share is time, \(t\).

The equations

First split the launch velocity \(v_0\) at angle \(\theta\) into components:

$$v_{0x} = v_0\cos\theta \qquad v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta$$

Horizontal: constant velocity

$$a_x = 0$$ $$v_x = v_0\cos\theta$$ $$x = v_0\cos\theta\;t$$

Vertical: constant acceleration

$$a_y = -g$$ $$v_y = v_0\sin\theta - g\,t$$ $$y = h + v_0\sin\theta\;t - \tfrac12 g t^2$$

Shortcuts for landing at launch height (\(h = 0\))

$$T = \frac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g}$$

Time of flight

$$R = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}$$

Range

$$H = \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}$$

Maximum height

These only work when the projectile lands at the same height it was launched from. For a cliff or a table, use the full \(y\) equation and solve for \(t\).

Try it

Each button sets up the simulation and runs the launches for you. Predict the result first, then watch.

  • Same speed, 30° and 60°. Which one lands farther?
  • Hunt for the longest throw. 30°, 45° and 60° at 20 m/s from the ground.
  • From a 30 m cliff. Is 45° still the best angle?
  • The same throw on the Moon. Gravity is about one-sixth as strong.

What you should see: 30° and 60° tie, because \(\sin 60^\circ = \sin 120^\circ\). 45° wins on flat ground. From the cliff, the lower 30° throw goes farther (43.6 m against 40.1 m) because the ball has extra time to fall.

Worked examples

1. A soccer kick

A ball is kicked from the ground at 20 m/s, 35° above the horizontal. Find its flight time, range and maximum height.

  1. Components: \(v_{0x} = 20\cos 35^\circ = 16.38\) m/s and \(v_{0y} = 20\sin 35^\circ = 11.47\) m/s.
  2. Flight time: \(T = \dfrac{2(11.47)}{9.81} = \) 2.34 s
  3. Range: \(R = v_{0x}T = 16.38 \times 2.34 = \) 38.3 m
  4. Maximum height: \(H = \dfrac{11.47^2}{2(9.81)} = \) 6.71 m

2. Thrown off a cliff

A stone is thrown horizontally at 15 m/s from the top of a 20 m cliff. How long is it in the air, and how far from the base does it land?

  1. Thrown horizontally means \(\theta = 0\), so \(v_{0y} = 0\) and \(v_{0x} = 15\) m/s.
  2. Vertical: \(0 = 20 - \tfrac12(9.81)t^2\), so \(t = \sqrt{2(20)/9.81} = \) 2.02 s
  3. Horizontal: \(x = 15 \times 2.02 = \) 30.3 m

Common mistakes

  • Using \(v_0\) in the x or y equation. Always use the component, \(v_0\cos\theta\) or \(v_0\sin\theta\).
  • Saying the velocity is zero at the top. Only \(v_y\) is zero. The ball is still moving sideways at \(v_0\cos\theta\).
  • Using the range shortcut from a cliff. \(R = v_0^2\sin 2\theta / g\) assumes the ball lands at launch height.
  • Mixing up signs. Pick up as positive and keep it that way. Then \(a_y = -9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\) for the whole flight, on the way up too.

Quick check

1. At the highest point of its path, a projectile's velocity is…

At the top, \(v_y = 0\) but \(v_x\) never changes, so the velocity points sideways.

2. Two balls leave the ground at the same speed, one at 30° and one at 60°. Which lands farther away?

Range depends on \(\sin 2\theta\), and \(\sin 60^\circ = \sin 120^\circ\). Complementary angles give equal ranges.

3. From the same height, one ball is dropped and another is thrown horizontally at the same instant. Which hits the ground first?

Both start with \(v_{0y} = 0\) and fall with the same \(g\). Horizontal speed has no effect on the vertical motion.

4. On flat ground, you double the launch speed and keep the angle the same. The range becomes…

\(R \propto v_0^2\), so \(2^2 = 4\). Try 10 m/s and 20 m/s in the simulation.

This lesson ignores air resistance, which is a good model for heavy, slow objects over short distances.
