Sets, lesson 3

Union, Intersection and Counting

Some students play cricket, some play football, and some play both. To count them without counting anyone twice, you need the union and the intersection of two sets.

Download slides (.pptx) 12 slides with teacher notes

Intersection of two sets

Intersection. The set of all elements common to set \(A\) and set \(B\) is the intersection of \(A\) and \(B\), written \(A \cap B\) and read “\(A\) intersection \(B\)”. $$A \cap B = \{x \mid x \in A \text{ and } x \in B\}$$

For \(A = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\}\) and \(B = \{2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12\}\), \(A \cap B = \{2, 4, 6, 12\}\). In a Venn diagram it is the part where the circles overlap. If \(A \cap B = \varnothing\), the sets have nothing in common and are called disjoint sets.

Properties: \(A \cap B = B \cap A\); \(A \cap A = A\); \(A \cap \varnothing = \varnothing\); \(A \cap A' = \varnothing\); and if \(A \subseteq B\) then \(A \cap B = A\).

Union of two sets

Union. The set of all elements that are in \(A\) or in \(B\) (or in both) is the union of \(A\) and \(B\), written \(A \cup B\) and read “\(A\) union \(B\)”. $$A \cup B = \{x \mid x \in A \text{ or } x \in B\}$$

For the same sets, \(A \cup B = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12\}\). The common elements 2, 4, 6 and 12 are written only once.

Properties: \(A \cup B = B \cup A\); \(A \cup A = A\); \(A \cup \varnothing = A\); \(A \cup A' = U\); and if \(A \subseteq B\) then \(A \cup B = B\).

Shade \((A \cup B)'\) and then \(A' \cap B'\) in the simulation: they are the same region. In the same way \((A \cap B)' = A' \cup B'\). These two rules are called De Morgan’s laws.

Number of elements in a union

Formula. For any two finite sets \(A\) and \(B\), $$n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B)$$

Why subtract? The elements in \(A \cap B\) are counted once in \(n(A)\) and again in \(n(B)\). Taking \(n(A \cap B)\) away once means every element is counted exactly once. For the sets above: \(6 + 6 - 4 = 8 = n(A \cup B)\).

Word problems

Draw a Venn diagram with four regions: only \(A\), both, only \(B\), and neither. Fill in the both region first, because the other regions are found by subtracting it.

  • Only \(A\): \(n(A) - n(A \cap B)\)
  • Only \(B\): \(n(B) - n(A \cap B)\)
  • Neither: \(n(U) - n(A \cup B)\)

Choose what to shade, and drag numbers to change A and B. For word problems the simulation switches to counts with sliders.

  • Intersection. Factors of 12 and even numbers up to 12.
  • Union. The same two sets.
  • Disjoint sets. Odd numbers and multiples of 4 up to 10.
  • De Morgan. Shade (A ∪ B)′, then A′ ∩ B′.
  • Word problem. 40 students: 25 play cricket, 20 football, 10 both.

What you should see: A ∩ B = {2, 4, 6, 12} and A ∪ B = {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12}; the check reads 6 + 6 − 4 = 8. The odd numbers and {4, 8} share nothing, so A ∩ B = ∅ and 5 + 2 − 0 = 7. (A ∪ B)′ and A′ ∩ B′ are both {5, 7, 9, 11}. In the class, 25 + 20 − 10 = 35 play a game, so 5 play neither.

Worked examples

1. Union, intersection and the formula

\(A = \{x \mid x \text{ is a factor of } 12\}\) and \(B = \{x \mid x \text{ is an even natural number}, x \le 12\}\). Find \(A \cap B\) and \(A \cup B\), and verify \(n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B)\).

  1. \(A = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\}\), \(B = \{2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12\}\).
  2. A ∩ B = {2, 4, 6, 12} and A ∪ B = {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12}
  3. \(n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B) = 6 + 6 - 4 = 8 = n(A \cup B)\) ✓

2. How many speak both?

In a group of 60 people, 38 speak Marathi and 30 speak Hindi. Everyone speaks at least one of the two. How many speak both? How many speak only Hindi?

  1. Let \(M\) and \(H\) be the sets. Everyone speaks at least one, so \(n(M \cup H) = 60\).
  2. \(60 = 38 + 30 - n(M \cap H)\), so \(n(M \cap H) = 68 - 60 = \) 8 speak both
  3. Only Hindi: \(30 - 8 = \) 22

3. How many have neither?

In a survey of 70 families, 42 have a two-wheeler, 25 have a car and 15 have both. How many families have neither?

  1. \(n(A \cup B) = 42 + 25 - 15 = 52\) families have at least one.
  2. Neither: \(70 - 52 = \) 18 families

Common mistakes

  • Writing common elements twice. In A ∪ B each element appears once: {2, 3} ∪ {3, 5} = {2, 3, 5}.
  • Just adding n(A) and n(B). That counts the overlap twice. Subtract n(A ∩ B) once.
  • Mixing up ∩ and ∪. ∩ means “and” (common elements only); ∪ means “or” (everything in either set).
  • Using n(A) for “only A”. Only A is n(A) − n(A ∩ B). Fill the overlap first.

Quick check

1. A = {2, 3, 5} and B = {3, 5, 7}. A ∪ B is…

Every element of either set, each written once. {3, 5} is A ∩ B.

2. n(A) = 15, n(B) = 12 and n(A ∩ B) = 5. Then n(A ∪ B) is…

15 + 12 − 5 = 22.

3. If A ⊆ B, then A ∩ B is…

Every element of A is already in B, so the common part is all of A.

4. In a class of 50, each student likes tea or coffee or both. 30 like tea and 25 like coffee. How many like both?

n(T ∪ C) = 50, so n(T ∩ C) = 30 + 25 − 50 = 5.
