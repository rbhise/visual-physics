Financial Planning, lesson 1

Savings and Investment

Money comes in, money goes out. Financial planning means deciding in advance how much to spend, how much to save, and where to keep the savings so that they grow safely.

Income, expenditure and savings

The money a family earns is its income. The money it spends on food, rent, electricity, education, travel and so on is its expenditure. What is left over is savings.

Savings = Income − Expenditure. Planning in advance how the income will be spent and saved is called financial planning. A written plan of income and expenditure is a budget.

Savings are often written as a percentage of income. If a family earns ₹30,000 a month and spends ₹25,500, it saves ₹4,500, which is \(\dfrac{4500}{30000} \times 100 = 15\%\) of its income.

Investment

Savings kept at home do not grow. Putting savings somewhere that pays interest or may increase in value is called investment. Before investing, think about three things:

  • Safety: is the money safe, or could some of it be lost?
  • Returns: how much will it earn?
  • Liquidity: how quickly can you take the money out when you need it?
Where to investWhat you getRisk
Bank: savings account, fixed deposit, recurring depositInterest at a rate fixed by the bankLow
Post office schemes (savings account, NSC, PPF and others)Interest at a rate fixed by the governmentLow
Gold, land and housesTheir price may rise over time; no fixed interestMedium: prices go up and down
Shares of companiesA share of the company's profit; the share price may rise or fallHigh
Mutual fundsMany people's money, invested by experts in shares and other optionsDepends on the fund

The rule of thumb is: the higher the possible return, the higher the risk. That is why people usually spread their savings over several options.

Interest: the tool for comparing

For a principal \(P\), a rate of \(R\%\) per year and \(N\) years:

$$\text{Simple interest } I = \frac{PNR}{100} \qquad\qquad \text{Compound interest: amount } A = P\left(1 + \frac{R}{100}\right)^{N}$$

In compound interest, each year's interest is added to the principal, and the next year's interest is worked out on the bigger amount. To compare two investments of different sizes, compare the percentage gain: \(\dfrac{\text{gain}}{\text{amount invested}} \times 100\).

Worked examples

1. Income from savings

Ravi saves 15% of his monthly income. His savings are ₹3,600 a month. What is his monthly income?

  1. Let the income be \(x\). Then \(15\%\) of \(x\) is 3,600: \(\dfrac{15}{100}x = 3600\).
  2. \(x = \dfrac{3600 \times 100}{15} = 24000\).
  3. Income ₹24,000; his expenditure is ₹20,400.

2. Which investment gained more?

Asha puts ₹50,000 in a fixed deposit at 8% per year compound interest for 2 years. Neha buys shares for ₹30,000 and sells them after 2 years for ₹36,000. Whose investment gained more, as a percentage?

  1. Asha: \(A = 50000\left(1 + \tfrac{8}{100}\right)^2 = 50000 \times 1.1664 = 58320\). Gain \(= 8320\), which is \(\tfrac{8320}{50000} \times 100 = 16.64\%\).
  2. Neha: gain \(= 36000 - 30000 = 6000\), which is \(\tfrac{6000}{30000} \times 100 = 20\%\).
  3. Neha's gain was higher: 20% against 16.64%. But her shares could also have fallen; Asha's deposit could not.

3. Splitting an investment

Kiran invests ₹40,000 in two schemes: part at 6% and the rest at 8% simple interest per year. Her total interest for one year is ₹2,900. How much did she put in each?

  1. Let the amounts be \(x\) and \(y\). Then \(x + y = 40000\) and \(\dfrac{6x}{100} + \dfrac{8y}{100} = 2900\), that is \(6x + 8y = 290000\).
  2. Multiply the first by 6: \(6x + 6y = 240000\). Subtract: \(2y = 50000\), so \(y = 25000\) and \(x = 15000\).
  3. ₹15,000 at 6% and ₹25,000 at 8%. Check: \(900 + 2000 = 2900\) ✓

Common mistakes

  • Percentage of the wrong total. Savings % is savings ÷ income × 100, not savings ÷ expenditure.
  • Compound interest on the first principal every year. Each year's interest is on the amount at the start of that year.
  • Comparing rupees, not percentages. ₹8,320 on ₹50,000 is a smaller gain than ₹6,000 on ₹30,000.
  • Ignoring risk. A higher return is not guaranteed; shares and gold can also fall in value.

Quick check

1. A family earns ₹40,000 a month and spends ₹34,000. What percentage of its income does it save?

Savings = 40,000 − 34,000 = ₹6,000, and 6,000 ÷ 40,000 × 100 = 15%.

2. ₹20,000 is invested at 10% per year compound interest. What is the amount after 2 years?

A = 20,000 × 1.1 × 1.1 = ₹24,200. (₹24,000 is the simple interest amount.)

3. Which of these investments carries the highest risk?

A share's price can rise or fall, so the money invested is not fixed. The other three pay a fixed rate of interest.

4. Meera saves 12% of her income, which is ₹4,800. What is her income?

12% of x = 4,800, so x = 4,800 × 100 ÷ 12 = ₹40,000.
