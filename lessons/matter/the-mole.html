A counting unit for particles

Atoms and molecules are so small that even a spoonful of water contains an enormous number of them. Chemists count them in groups called moles.

One mole of a substance contains as many particles (atoms, molecules or ions) as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12. That number is Avogadro's number: \(N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\).

Molar mass

The mass of one mole of a substance, in grams, is its molar mass (M), measured in g/mol. It has the same number value as the molecular mass in u:

  • Water: molecular mass 18 u, so 1 mol of water is 18 g.
  • Carbon: atomic mass 12 u, so 1 mol of carbon is 12 g.
  • Glucose: 180 u, so 1 mol of glucose is 180 g.

So one mole of every substance contains the same number of particles, but has a different mass, because the particles have different masses.

The two mole formulae

$$n = \frac{m}{M} \qquad\qquad N = n \times N_A$$

Here \(n\) is the number of moles, \(m\) the mass of the sample in grams, \(M\) the molar mass and \(N\) the number of particles. To count atoms rather than molecules, multiply by the number of atoms in each molecule.

Try it

Each bag in the simulation holds one mole. Compare the masses of one bag of each substance.

  • 36 g of water. How many moles?
  • 12 g of carbon. Exactly one mole.
  • 4.4 g of carbon dioxide. Less than one bag.
  • 180 g of glucose. A big mass for one mole.

What you should see: 36 g of water is 2 mol, or 1.20 × 10²⁴ molecules. 12 g of carbon is exactly 1 mol, 6.02 × 10²³ atoms. 4.4 g of carbon dioxide is 0.1 mol. 180 g of glucose is also just 1 mol, because each glucose molecule is 10 times heavier than a water molecule.

Worked examples

1. Moles of water

How many moles and how many molecules are there in 36 g of water?

  1. \(M = 18\) g/mol, so \(n = 36 / 18 = \) 2 mol
  2. \(N = 2 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = \) 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules

2. Atoms in carbon dioxide

How many molecules and how many atoms are in 4.4 g of carbon dioxide?

  1. \(M = 44\) g/mol, so \(n = 4.4 / 44 = 0.1\) mol
  2. Molecules \(= 0.1 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = \) 6.022 × 10²² molecules
  3. Each CO₂ has 3 atoms: \(3 \times 6.022 \times 10^{22} = \) 1.807 × 10²³ atoms

3. From particles to mass

What is the mass of 3.011 × 10²³ atoms of sodium? (Na = 23)

  1. \(n = \dfrac{3.011 \times 10^{23}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}} = 0.5\) mol
  2. \(m = n \times M = 0.5 \times 23 = \) 11.5 g

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying instead of dividing. Moles = mass ÷ molar mass. 36 g of water is 36 ÷ 18 = 2 mol, not 36 × 18.
  • Counting molecules as atoms. 1 mol of H₂O has 6.022 × 10²³ molecules but 3 times as many atoms.
  • u and g/mol are different numbers. They have the same value: 18 u per molecule, 18 g per mole.
  • Dropping the power of ten. Write Avogadro's number in full: 6.022 × 10²³.

Quick check

1. How many moles are in 88 g of carbon dioxide (CO₂ = 44 g/mol)?

88 ÷ 44 = 2 mol.

2. One mole of any substance contains…

That is Avogadro's number.

3. What is the mass of 0.5 mol of water?

0.5 × 18 = 9 g.

4. Which contains more atoms: 1 mol of water or 1 mol of carbon?

Both have 6.022 × 10²³ particles, but each water molecule has 3 atoms.
