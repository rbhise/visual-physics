Spherical Mirrors and Ray Diagrams

The back of a steel spoon shows you small and upright; the inside shows you upside down, and up close, huge. Both are curved mirrors, and three simple rays explain everything they do.

Concave and convex mirrors

A spherical mirror is a piece cut from a hollow, shiny sphere. If the inside (the hollow side) reflects, it is a concave mirror. If the outside (the bulging side) reflects, it is a convex mirror.

Rays coming in parallel to each other meet after reflecting from a concave mirror, so it is called a converging mirror. A convex mirror spreads parallel rays apart: it is a diverging mirror.

Words you need

  • Pole (P): the centre of the mirror's reflecting surface.
  • Centre of curvature (C): the centre of the sphere the mirror is part of. Radius of curvature (R): the radius of that sphere, the distance PC.
  • Principal axis: the straight line through P and C.
  • Principal focus (F): the point on the axis where rays parallel to the axis meet after reflection (concave), or seem to come from (convex).
  • Focal length (f): the distance PF. For small mirrors \(f = R \div 2\), so F lies halfway between P and C.

Three rays to find an image

Light from the tip of an object goes in all directions, but three rays are easy to follow:

  • Ray 1 (teal): a ray parallel to the principal axis reflects through F (concave) or as if it came from F (convex).
  • Ray 2 (green): a ray through F (or heading towards F, for convex) reflects parallel to the axis.
  • Ray 3 (red): a ray through C (or heading towards C) hits the mirror at right angles and reflects straight back.

Where the reflected rays meet is the tip of the image. Any two rays are enough. If they really meet, the image is real and inverted, and can be caught on a screen. If they only seem to meet behind the mirror (dashed lines), it is virtual and erect.

Images formed by a convex mirror

Wherever the object is, a convex mirror gives an image that is virtual, erect and diminished, behind the mirror between P and F. The closer the object, the bigger the image, but it never gets as big as the object.

Uses

  • Concave: shaving and make-up mirrors and dentists' mirrors (object inside F gives a large upright image); torches, car headlamps and searchlights (bulb at F sends out a parallel beam); solar cookers and furnaces (sunlight is concentrated at F).
  • Convex: rear-view mirrors of vehicles and mirrors at sharp bends or in shops, because they show a wide area in a small, upright image.

Try it

Pick a mirror and move the object. Watch where the coloured rays meet.

  • Walk the object in. Concave mirror: watch the image change at C and F.
  • Object at C. Compare the image with the object.
  • Inside F. Like a shaving mirror.
  • Convex mirror. Try any distance.

What you should see: Far away, the concave mirror's image is small, inverted and near F. It grows as the object comes in, is the same size at C, rushes off to infinity at F, and then jumps behind the mirror as a large upright virtual image. The convex mirror's image always stays small, upright and between P and F.

Worked examples

1. From R to f

A concave mirror has a radius of curvature of 36 cm. Where is its principal focus?

  1. \(f = R \div 2 = 36 \div 2 = \) 18 cm in front of the mirror, halfway between P and C

2. Predicting an image

An object stands 25 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 10 cm. Describe the image.

  1. C is at \(2f = 20\) cm, so the object is beyond C.
  2. The image is between F and C, real, inverted and diminished

3. Which mirror?

A mirror always gives a small, upright image, whatever the distance of the object. What kind of mirror is it, and where might you find one?

  1. Only a convex mirror always gives a virtual, erect, diminished image.
  2. It is used as a rear-view mirror in vehicles.

Common mistakes

  • “The focus is at C.” F is halfway between P and C: f = R ÷ 2.
  • “Concave mirrors always invert.” Inside F the image is virtual and erect.
  • Drawing the image behind a concave mirror for every case. Only when the object is between P and F.
  • “A convex mirror can magnify.” Its image is always smaller than the object.

Quick check

1. Rays parallel to the principal axis of a concave mirror meet after reflection at…

That point is the definition of the principal focus F.

2. Where should the bulb of a torch be placed in front of its concave mirror to give a parallel beam?

Rays from F reflect parallel to the axis.

3. An object is placed at C of a concave mirror. The image is…

A ray through C returns along itself, so the image forms at C, the same size and inverted.

4. Why are convex mirrors used as rear-view mirrors?

A convex mirror shows a wide area in a small, upright image.
