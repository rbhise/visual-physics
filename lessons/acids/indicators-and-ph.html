Acids, Bases, Indicators and pH

Lemon juice tastes sour, soap feels slippery, and a turmeric stain turns red when you wash it with soap. All three are clues to the same chemistry: acids and bases.

Acids and bases

According to the Swedish chemist Arrhenius:

  • An acid is a substance that gives H⁺ ions when dissolved in water. Example: \(\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{H}^+ + \text{Cl}^-\)
  • A base is a substance that gives OH⁻ ions when dissolved in water. Example: \(\text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{Na}^+ + \text{OH}^-\)

Bases that dissolve in water are called alkalis. Acids taste sour; bases taste bitter and feel soapy. Never taste or touch laboratory chemicals.

Strong and weak

A strong acid such as hydrochloric, sulphuric or nitric acid breaks up almost completely into ions in water. A weak acid such as acetic acid (in vinegar) or citric acid (in lemons) breaks up only partly. In the same way, sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide are strong bases, while ammonia solution is a weak base.

The pH scale

The pH scale measures how acidic or basic a solution is, usually on a range from 0 to 14:

  • pH less than 7: acidic. The lower the pH, the stronger the acid.
  • pH = 7: neutral, like pure water.
  • pH more than 7: basic. The higher the pH, the stronger the base.
Each step on the scale is a factor of ten. A solution of pH 4 has ten times as many H⁺ ions as one of pH 5, and a thousand times as many as pure water (pH 7). The readout shows this for every tube.

Try it

Switch indicators and look for the tubes that change colour.

  • Litmus. Which tubes turn blue?
  • Phenolphthalein. Which tubes turn pink?
  • Turmeric. Test the soap solution.
  • Universal indicator. See the whole scale at once.

What you should see: with litmus, the first four tubes are red, milk and water are purple, and the last four are blue. Phenolphthalein stays colourless until baking soda and beyond, which turn pink. Turmeric turns red-brown only in the basic tubes. Universal indicator gives a different colour for almost every tube, from red (pH 1) to violet (pH 13).

Worked examples

1. Reading the scale

Solution A has pH 3, solution B has pH 9 and solution C has pH 7. Classify each.

  1. A: pH below 7, so acidic
  2. B: pH above 7, so basic
  3. C: neutral

2. Ten times, a hundred times

Vinegar has a pH of about 3 and tomato juice about 4. How many times more H⁺ ions does vinegar have?

  1. They differ by 1 pH unit, so vinegar has 10 times as many H⁺ ions.
  2. Compared with pure water (pH 7), vinegar has \(10^{7-3} = \) 10 000 times as many.

3. Choosing an indicator

A student has a colourless solution and phenolphthalein. After adding a few drops, the solution stays colourless. Can she say the solution is acidic?

  1. Phenolphthalein is colourless in both acidic and neutral solutions.
  2. So no: the solution is either acidic or neutral. Litmus or universal indicator would tell them apart.

Common mistakes

  • “pH 6 is twice as acidic as pH 12.” pH is not a simple ruler. Each step is a factor of ten.
  • Lower pH means weaker acid. It is the other way: the lower the pH, the stronger the acid.
  • Phenolphthalein shows acids. It turns pink only in bases. It is colourless in acids and neutral solutions.
  • All bases are alkalis. Only bases that dissolve in water are alkalis.

Quick check

1. An acid, when dissolved in water, gives…

Arrhenius acids release H⁺ ions in water.

2. What colour does red litmus turn in soap solution?

Soap solution is basic, so it turns red litmus blue.

3. A solution has pH 12. It is…

pH well above 7 means strongly basic.

4. How many times more H⁺ ions does a solution of pH 2 have than one of pH 4?

Two pH steps: 10 × 10 = 100.
