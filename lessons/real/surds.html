Real Numbers, lesson 3

Surds and Rationalising

√72 looks messy, but a square of area 72 is just 36 small squares of area 2, so √72 = 6√2. Surds follow a few simple rules, and a clever multiplication clears them out of any denominator.

Surds and their order

Surd. Let \(n\) be a positive integer greater than 1 and \(a\) a positive rational number. If \(\sqrt[n]{a}\) is irrational, it is called a surd, and \(n\) is the order of the surd.

\(\sqrt[3]{7}\) is a surd of order 3, and \(\sqrt{5}\) is a surd of order 2. A surd of order 2 is called a quadratic surd; in this chapter we study only quadratic surds. \(\sqrt{49}\) is not a surd, because \(\sqrt{49} = 7\) is rational.

Simplest form

Take out the largest perfect-square factor: \(\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b}\).

$$\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \times 2} = \sqrt{36} \times \sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt{2}$$

The simulation shows why: a square of area 72 is made of \(6 \times 6\) small squares of area 2. Each small square has side \(\sqrt{2}\), so the big side is \(6\sqrt{2}\).

Like and unlike surds

Surds are like (similar) surds if, in simplest form, they have the same number under the root. \(\sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}\) and \(3\sqrt{5}\) are like surds. \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(\sqrt{3}\) are unlike surds.

Comparing surds

For positive numbers, the one with the bigger square is bigger. To compare \(4\sqrt{3}\) and \(3\sqrt{5}\), square them: \((4\sqrt{3})^2 = 16 \times 3 = 48\) and \((3\sqrt{5})^2 = 9 \times 5 = 45\). As \(48 > 45\), \(4\sqrt{3} > 3\sqrt{5}\).

Operations on surds

OperationRuleExample
Add, subtractonly like surds combine\(3\sqrt{2} + 5\sqrt{2} = 8\sqrt{2}\)
Multiply\(\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}\)\(\sqrt{6} \times \sqrt{15} = \sqrt{90} = 3\sqrt{10}\)
Divide\(\sqrt{a} \div \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{\tfrac{a}{b}}\)\(\sqrt{45} \div \sqrt{5} = \sqrt{9} = 3\)

Simplify first, then combine: \(\sqrt{50} - \sqrt{18} = 5\sqrt{2} - 3\sqrt{2} = 2\sqrt{2}\). But \(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\) cannot be simplified.

Rationalising the denominator

Rationalising factor. If the product of two surds is a rational number, each is called a rationalising factor of the other. \(\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{3} = 3\), so \(\sqrt{3}\) is a rationalising factor of \(\sqrt{3}\). (\(\sqrt{12}\) is one too, since \(\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{12} = 6\); \(\sqrt{3}\) is the simplest.)

To rationalise a denominator, multiply the numerator and the denominator by a rationalising factor of the denominator. The value does not change: \(\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{7}} = \dfrac{2 \times \sqrt{7}}{\sqrt{7} \times \sqrt{7}} = \dfrac{2\sqrt{7}}{7}\).

Binomial quadratic surds and the conjugate

\(\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}\) and \(5 + 2\sqrt{6}\) are binomial quadratic surds. The pair \(a + \sqrt{b}\) and \(a - \sqrt{b}\) are conjugate pairs. Their product is rational, because

$$(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}) = (\sqrt{a})^2 - (\sqrt{b})^2 = a - b$$

So the conjugate is a rationalising factor: \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{(\sqrt{6})^2 - (\sqrt{2})^2} = \dfrac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{4}\).

Worked examples

1. Simplify, then add

Simplify \(\sqrt{50} + \sqrt{32} - \sqrt{8}\).

  1. \(\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \times 2} = 5\sqrt{2}\), \(\sqrt{32} = \sqrt{16 \times 2} = 4\sqrt{2}\), \(\sqrt{8} = \sqrt{4 \times 2} = 2\sqrt{2}\).
  2. They are like surds: \(5\sqrt{2} + 4\sqrt{2} - 2\sqrt{2}\).
  3. \(7\sqrt{2}\)

2. Compare two surds

Which is greater, \(5\sqrt{2}\) or \(3\sqrt{6}\)?

  1. \((5\sqrt{2})^2 = 25 \times 2 = 50\).
  2. \((3\sqrt{6})^2 = 9 \times 6 = 54\).
  3. \(50 < 54\), so \(3\sqrt{6}\) is greater.

3. Surds on top and bottom

Rationalise the denominator of \(\dfrac{\sqrt{7} - \sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{7} + \sqrt{3}}\).

  1. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate \(\sqrt{7} - \sqrt{3}\).
  2. Denominator: \((\sqrt{7})^2 - (\sqrt{3})^2 = 7 - 3 = 4\).
  3. Numerator: \((\sqrt{7} - \sqrt{3})^2 = 7 - 2\sqrt{21} + 3 = 10 - 2\sqrt{21}\).
  4. \(\dfrac{10 - 2\sqrt{21}}{4} = \) \(\dfrac{5 - \sqrt{21}}{2}\)

Common mistakes

  • Splitting a root over a sum. √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5, not √9 + √16 = 7. Only products split: √(ab) = √a × √b.
  • Adding unlike surds. √2 + √3 is not √5. Only like surds combine, like apples with apples.
  • Multiplying by the same binomial. For 2 + √3 use the conjugate 2 − √3. Multiplying by 2 + √3 leaves a surd behind.
  • Changing only the denominator. Multiply the numerator and the denominator by the same factor, or the value changes.

Quick check

1. The order of the surd \(\sqrt[3]{10}\) is…

The order is the root taken: the cube root, so 3.

2. The simplest form of \(\sqrt{98}\) is…

98 = 49 × 2, so √98 = √49 × √2 = 7√2.

3. Which is greater?

(2√7)² = 28 and (3√3)² = 27. 28 > 27.

4. A rationalising factor of \(4 + \sqrt{3}\) is…

The conjugate: (4 + √3)(4 − √3) = 16 − 3 = 13, a rational number.
