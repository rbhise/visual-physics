Study of Sound, lesson 3

Echo and Reverberation

Shout across a valley and the hills shout back. Clap in a small room and you hear nothing extra. Both are reflections of sound; the difference is timing.

Download slides (.pptx) 12 slides with teacher notes

Sound reflects

Sound bounces off hard surfaces such as walls, cliffs and buildings, just as light bounces off a mirror, and it obeys the same laws of reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, and the incident sound, the reflected sound and the normal lie in one plane. You can check this with two long tubes pointed at a wall: the ticking of a watch at one tube is heard loudest through the other when the two make equal angles with the normal.

Echo

An echo is a sound heard again after it reflects from a distant surface. Our ears hold on to a sound for about 0.1 s. If the reflected sound returns sooner than that, it blends with the original and we do not hear it separately.

The sound travels to the wall and back, a distance \(2d\), in time \(t\):

$$2d = v\,t \quad\Rightarrow\quad d = \frac{v\,t}{2}$$

For a distinct echo \(t\) must be at least 0.1 s. In air at 20 °C (344 m/s) that needs \(d \ge 344 \times 0.1 \div 2 = 17.2\) m.

Reverberation

In a closed hall, sound reflects again and again from the walls, ceiling and floor. The many reflections overlap and make the sound linger after the source stops. This is reverberation. A little makes music sound full; too much makes speech blurred and hard to follow. Halls reduce it with sound-absorbing materials: curtains, carpets, padded seats, fibreboard and acoustic panels on the walls and ceiling.

Using reflected sound

  • Megaphones and horns are flared tubes: repeated reflections send the sound forward in one direction instead of spreading it.
  • Stethoscope: the sound of the heartbeat reflects along the tube to the doctor's ears.
  • Curved ceilings and sound boards: a curved ceiling, or a curved board behind a speaker on stage, reflects sound evenly to every part of the hall.

Worked examples

1. The shortest distance

What is the least distance from a wall at which you can hear a distinct echo in air at 20 °C?

  1. \(v = 332 + 0.6 \times 20 = 344\) m/s; \(t\) must be at least 0.1 s
  2. \(d = v t / 2 = 344 \times 0.1 / 2 = \) 17.2 m

2. Distance from the echo time

A boy claps facing a building and hears the echo 0.5 s later. The air is at 20 °C. How far is the building?

  1. \(d = v t / 2 = 344 \times 0.5 / 2 = \) 86 m

3. Speed from an echo

A girl stands 165 m from a cliff and claps. The echo comes back after 1 s. Find the speed of sound.

  1. The sound travels \(2 \times 165 = 330\) m in 1 s
  2. \(v = 330 \div 1 = \) 330 m/s (a cold day, just below 0 °C)

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to halve. The sound goes there and back: d = vt ÷ 2.
  • “Every reflection is an echo.” Only one that returns 0.1 s or more after the original is heard separately.
  • “17.2 m always.” That value is for 344 m/s; in colder air the minimum distance is a little less.
  • “Reverberation is always bad.” A little makes music sound full; too much blurs speech.

Quick check

1. For a distinct echo, the reflected sound must reach us at least…

Our ears retain a sound for about 0.1 s.

2. An echo is heard 2 s after a shout. The speed of sound is 340 m/s. How far away is the reflecting cliff?

d = 340 × 2 ÷ 2 = 340 m.

3. Which of these reduces reverberation in a hall?

Soft, porous materials absorb sound instead of reflecting it.

4. Why does a stethoscope work?

Multiple reflections carry the heartbeat sound along the tube.
