Sound Waves

Touch your throat while you hum and you feel it buzz. Every sound starts with something vibrating, and the vibration travels to your ear through the air as a wave.

Sound comes from vibration

A struck tuning fork, a plucked guitar string, the skin of a drum and your vocal cords all vibrate: they move rapidly back and forth. A vibrating object pushes on the particles of the air next to it, those push on their neighbours, and the disturbance passes along. That travelling disturbance is sound.

Sound needs a medium

The material that carries sound (air, water, a wall, a rail) is called the medium. If an electric bell rings inside a glass jar and the air is slowly pumped out, the ringing grows fainter and finally cannot be heard, even though you can see the hammer still striking. With no particles to pass the vibration on, sound cannot travel through a vacuum. That is why astronauts on a spacewalk talk by radio.

Longitudinal waves

In air, each particle moves back and forth along the direction in which the sound travels. Where particles crowd together the air is squeezed: a compression (C). Where they spread apart it is thinned: a rarefaction (R). A wave in which the particles move along the direction of travel is a longitudinal wave. On a stretched string or rope the particles move up and down, at right angles to the direction of travel: a transverse wave with crests and troughs.

The particles do not travel with the sound. Each one only vibrates about its own place; what travels is the pattern, and with it the energy.

Describing a wave

  • Wavelength (λ): the distance between two neighbouring compressions (or rarefactions). Unit: metre.
  • Frequency (ν): the number of vibrations each second. Unit: hertz (Hz); 1 Hz = one vibration per second.
  • Time period (T): the time for one vibration. \(T = 1/\nu\).
  • Amplitude (A): the largest change from the undisturbed state, such as the greatest squeezing of the air.

In one time period the wave moves forward by one wavelength, so its speed is

$$v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = \nu \lambda$$

Pitch and loudness

Pitch depends on frequency: a higher frequency gives a higher, shriller note (a whistle, a bird's song); a lower frequency gives a deeper note (a lion's roar, a drum). Loudness depends on amplitude: the larger the amplitude, the more energy the wave carries and the louder the sound.

Worked examples

1. Wavelength from frequency

A tuning fork vibrates at 500 Hz. Sound travels at 340 m/s in air. Find the wavelength and the time period.

  1. \(\lambda = v / \nu = 340 / 500 = \) 0.68 m
  2. \(T = 1 / \nu = 1 / 500 = \) 0.002 s (2 milliseconds)

2. Frequency from wavelength

A sound in air (340 m/s) has a wavelength of 1.7 m. What is its frequency? Is it high or low pitched?

  1. \(\nu = v / \lambda = 340 / 1.7 = \) 200 Hz
  2. That is a low, deep note.

3. How far in 3 seconds?

A sound wave has a frequency of 850 Hz and a wavelength of 0.4 m. How far does it travel in 3 s?

  1. \(v = \nu \lambda = 850 \times 0.4 = 340\) m/s
  2. Distance \(= v \times t = 340 \times 3 = \) 1020 m

Common mistakes

  • “The air travels to your ear.” Each particle only vibrates in place. The disturbance travels.
  • “Higher pitch travels faster.” In one medium all frequencies travel at the same speed; a higher ν just means a shorter λ.
  • Mixing up pitch and loudness. Pitch goes with frequency; loudness goes with amplitude.
  • “Sound can cross space.” Sound needs a medium. It cannot travel through a vacuum.

Quick check

1. In a longitudinal sound wave, the particles of air…

Particles vibrate about their places along the line in which the wave travels.

2. A sound has a frequency of 680 Hz in air (340 m/s). Its wavelength is…

λ = v ÷ ν = 340 ÷ 680 = 0.5 m.

3. Which property of a sound decides its loudness?

Larger amplitude means more energy and a louder sound.

4. Why can't astronauts hear each other directly on the Moon?

The Moon has no atmosphere, and sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
